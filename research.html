Research

Temperature, disease, and the winter lives of bats

White-nose syndrome is caused by the fungus Pseudogymnoascus destructans (Pd), which grows faster in warmer conditions. My dissertation asks whether colder hibernacula protect bats from the disease, tests that idea experimentally, and studies how bats use and move among hibernacula across the winter.

01

Temperature and white-nose syndrome

Little brown bat (Myotis lucifugus) populations collapsed after white-nose syndrome reached Michigan, but not evenly. Using 350 winter surveys at 48 hibernacula in the Upper Peninsula, we fit Bayesian models of declines and persistence and found that cooler hibernacula buffered little brown bats from the impacts of the disease.

The result points to hibernaculum temperature as something managers could protect or even modify.

Status: Published in Conservation Biology (2026)

With: J. D. Wolfe, A. Kurta, S. M. Smith, J. E. DePue

02

Experimentally cooling a hibernaculum

Mead Adit at Carp Lake Mine holds a stable winter temperature near 7.5 °C, comfortable for bats but also for Pd. With engineer Joe Beeson, we built a fan-and-duct system that draws cold winter air into a treatment section of the mine, lowering its temperature by several degrees while staying within what bats tolerate.

The experiment tests whether the landscape pattern from Chapter 1 holds when temperature is changed directly. The first winter (2025–26) is complete, and the system is being upgraded for the next season.

Partners: Michigan DNR; Porcupine Mountains Wilderness State Park

Funding: Ecosystem Science Center, Michigan Tech

03

Winter movement between hibernacula

Hibernation was long treated as a period of near stillness, yet some bats leave one hibernaculum mid-winter and settle in another. If hibernacula behave as a connected network, that matters for disease spread, for recovery, and for how risks such as wind energy development are assessed.

I combine repeated within-winter surveys with individual marking. Each bat receives a numbered forearm band, and I developed an anodizing process that colors bands by hibernaculum, so a bat's site of origin can be read from a distance without handling it again.

Status: Data collection ongoing (2025–2027)

With: J. D. Wolfe, B. R. Gusick, J. E. DePue (Michigan DNR)

04

Roost microclimate and habitat selection

Within a single mine, temperature and humidity can vary sharply from the entrance to the deepest workings. I measure rock and air temperature and humidity where bats roost and where they do not, to compare how little brown bats and big brown bats (Eptesicus fuscus) select thermal habitat at two spatial scales.

Acoustic detectors and cameras at mine entrances add a record of when bats are active through the winter.

Status: Manuscript in preparation

With: J. D. Wolfe, B. R. Gusick, J. E. DePue

A manager's guide to cooling a hibernaculum

I am writing a practical guide for wildlife managers and mine operators on whether and how to cool a bat hibernaculum, drawing on the regional analysis, the Mead Adit experiment, and other cooling projects.
